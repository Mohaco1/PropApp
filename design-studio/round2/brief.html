Round 2: back to the drawing board

The reasoning behind the five new concepts, an honest critique of Round 1, and how I'd score the results. All five use the same fictional scenario: Harbourview Pharmacy on Thursday 19 November.

1. What your feedback actually says

I read your 63 studio notes and our earlier conversations as evidence of underlying needs, not as rules.

Consistent likes

  • Seeing a lot at once, but only when it's ordered (Home A "can see a lot", D4's task set-up)
  • Progress you can see (Home A's bar, Question C's segmented bar)
  • Clear rows with status on the right (Field Kit's structure won every time)
  • Calm, light colour with some warmth (Home C)
  • Personal touches: your name, the initials circle
  • Strong typography (D1 question: "veeeery nice")
  • Features that save effort: board tabs, snap evidence, legal alerts, the year plan, the month calendar

Consistent dislikes

  • "Too busy" (Home B, the audit grid, Field Ledger, meeting notes, search)
  • "Chaotic" (the current Services and History pages)
  • Dark-and-yellow, "in your face" colour
  • iPhone-only patterns (toggles); A/B/C letters; icons inside answers
  • Anything that adds work without value ("Listen to SOP")
  • Bland grey with no character (D4's colour)

The needs underneath

  • "Too busy" is about hierarchy, not density. You liked Home A, which is dense, and disliked Home B, which has similar content. The difference is whether one thing clearly leads. So I haven't banned density: Concept B is very dense, but strictly ordered.
  • Dark-and-yellow was rejected for its execution, not because dark is wrong. Field Kit's structure won every time, so the outlined, high-contrast row idea survives in several concepts without the hi-vis palette.
  • "Not just an iOS app" is about trust and familiarity. Tick boxes and plain radio choices read as "a serious record", while toggles read as "a phone setting". All five concepts use tick boxes and radio choices.
  • A contradiction: you want both "see a lot on one page" and "minimalism". I've split that into different philosophies (A minimal, B dense) rather than averaging it into a compromise you'd find bland.
  • You were 50/50 on the SOP preview. My view is that the idea (show what an answer changes) is right but was in the wrong place. In Round 2 it lives on the Policies page, where you approve the document.

2. Honest critique of Round 1

  • Mostly cosmetic. Designs 1–6 shared the same structure (header, counts, list, bottom tabs) and changed only colour, font and corner radius. That's why mixing them felt natural, and why nothing felt like a breakthrough.
  • Generic SaaS defaults: four count tiles, a card for everything, small uppercase labels above headings, a bottom tab bar on every screen.
  • Built screen by screen, not workflow by workflow. No design followed a real job from start to finish, such as "Sam says it's fixed, so check it and close it".
  • Too much of the same weight. Badges, pills, borders and colour all competed, which is what "busy" usually means.
  • Ignored where the proprietor actually is: in the car, on the shop floor, or at a laptop in the evening. Most designs suited none of these especially well.
  • Multi-pharmacy was an afterthought. It appeared in one flow (F3), which you rightly found complicated.

3. Principles for Round 2

  1. One thing leads on every screen.
  2. Design workflows, not screens: every concept covers the same seven areas end to end.
  3. Status in words first, colour second (accessibility, and calmer).
  4. Records feel like records: tick boxes, radio choices, dates, who and when.
  5. Help, don't police: plain sentences, suggestions instead of warnings, and nothing ever closed or approved for you.
  6. The same data in every concept, so you compare ideas rather than content.

4. The five concepts

A · Quiet Precision

ConservativePhone and laptop

Typography and spacing do the work instead of boxes. Today is a single "Needs you" list ordered by urgency, under a one-sentence summary. Details open in place, so you rarely change screen. The audit is a clean radio form, and keyboard shortcuts are there on a laptop.

Why: it fixes "busy" by subtracting rather than restyling, and it's the closest to the current app's model, so it's the lowest risk.

B · Command Centre

DenseLaptop first

A workspace: a rail with both pharmacies and their counts, an attention queue as a table, a detail pane beside it, a side-by-side pharmacy matrix, ⌘K jump-to search, answering audits with keys 1–4, and a staff credentials matrix.

Why: it tests whether "see a lot at once" works when it's strictly ordered, and it treats multiple pharmacies as first class.

C · Guided Path

Task firstPhone first

There's no dashboard to interpret. The app gives you the next step, then the rest of today's path. Every job is a short guided flow: the visit is one question at a time with automatic next and Undo; "Is the fridge log fixed?" is a yes/no; registration and SOP verification become small checklists. Records sit behind one switch.

Why: it fixes "busy" by showing one thing at a time, which suits a proprietor with ten spare minutes between other jobs.

D · Editorial

PremiumDistinctive

The app reads like a daily briefing: a headline that summarises the day, big figures separated by rules, a register-style table, ruled observation notes, an almanac of dates, and SOPs shown as typeset documents with highlighted lines.

Why: it tests whether character can come from typography and writing rather than colour, answering "bland" without being "in your face".

E · The Floor (wildcard)

UnconventionalSpatial

The pharmacy's floor plan is the navigation. Reviews, actions, procedures and staff responsibilities are pinned to the areas they belong to. "Lenses" switch what the map shows, and a site visit is a route you walk from area to area.

Why: every other design assumes the app is lists. Proprietors oversee a place, and on a visit they literally walk it.

Strengths and weaknesses

  • A: clearest and calmest, and it works on any screen. Its risk is feeling plain on a large laptop screen, where it deliberately uses a narrow column.
  • B: fastest for an experienced user, and the only concept where both pharmacies are truly side by side. It's demanding on a phone, and new users may find it a lot.
  • C: the most supportive, and best when you're short on time. It's weaker when you want to browse or plan, and its "path" logic needs careful rules so it never feels like it's making decisions for you.
  • D: the most memorable and premium. Long lists and frequent use favour scanning over reading, so it's slower day to day.
  • E: the most original, and genuinely useful during a site visit. As the main navigation it's hard to make accessible, you'd need to draw a floor plan for every pharmacy, and it doesn't scale to things with no place, such as insurance.

A practical note on feasibility: the real app blocks external fonts for security (its Content Security Policy), so the Google fonts in these prototypes would become system fonts or fonts embedded in the file. That matters most for D, whose character depends on its serif.

My recommendation as lead designer

Use A (Quiet Precision) as the foundation. It best fixes "busy" and suits a phone, it's accessible, and it fits the current app with the least risk.

Then, after you've tried each concept on its own, consider borrowing three specific ideas:

  • C's guided flows for the jobs where guidance matters: the site visit, "Is it fixed?" and verifying answers.
  • B's queue-and-detail layout as the laptop version of A, when we get to laptop.
  • E's walk-the-floor route as an optional mode inside the site visit, not as the main navigation.

D's one-sentence summary of the day is already part of A. Please try each concept on its own first, then tell me which ideas you'd keep.
